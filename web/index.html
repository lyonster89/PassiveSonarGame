▼ going deeper · why the rays curve

How sound bends

Sound in the sea does not travel in straight lines. It bends toward water where it moves more slowly. Its speed depends on temperature and pressure, so it changes with depth: fast near the warm surface, slowest about a kilometre down, then faster again as the pressure builds.

That is why the rays in the ocean view curve. Shallow rays sink, turn around in the deep and climb back to the surface tens of kilometres away. Between where they leave and where they return, no direct sound arrives. That gap is a shadow zone. Steeper rays reach into it only by bouncing off the sea floor, which costs loudness.

Tracing rays…

Try it: move the listener deeper, or change the depth where sound is slowest, and watch the clear zones move. A contact in a clear zone gives a sharp bearing. One in a shadow zone gives a blurred one, or none.

The ocean here is Munk's standard deep-water profile, 5,000 m deep with a flat floor. Each ray is integrated with fourth-order Runge-Kutta in 100 m steps. On a sea-floor bounce a ray is drawn lighter, and it is dropped after two bounces.

▼ going deeper · what the waterfall can and cannot tell you

Reading the waterfall

A bearing is the compass direction a sound arrives from. The waterfall plots one bearing every 30 seconds, newest on top. A strong signal draws a thin line. A weak one smears, because its direction is harder to pin down.

Here is the catch. Bearings alone do not give range. A slow ship nearby and a fast ship far away can draw exactly the same trace, as the chart on this layer shows.

The way out is to move. When the listener changes course, each candidate track would have shifted the bearings differently, and only one of them still fits. That is why turning your ship in the hunt makes the teal cloud collapse.

▼ going deeper · how the computer makes its estimate

The tracking math

The teal cloud is 6,000 guesses. Each one is a complete story about the contact: where it is, which way it is going and how fast. Every 30 seconds:

  1. Each guess moves forward along its own course.
  2. Each guess works out the bearing it would have produced.
  3. Each guess is scored by how close that is to the bearing actually measured.
  4. Poor guesses are dropped. Good ones are copied and nudged slightly, so the cloud keeps exploring.

weight ∝ exp( −½ · (bearing error ÷ σ)² )

Start a hunt to see the filter's numbers here.

This is a particle filter. It suits the problem because the uncertainty starts as a long thin wedge along the first bearing, not a tidy ellipse, and simpler filters handle that shape badly.

σ, the blur on each bearing, comes from the passive sonar equation. Signal strength is the source's loudness, minus what the ocean takes away over the path, minus background noise, plus the gain of the listening array.

signal excess = SL − TL − NL + DI

The nudge in step 4 is drawn from the cloud's own shape (its 4-by-4 covariance, scaled by a kernel bandwidth), a method called regularised resampling.

▼ sea floor · how it is built and checked

Engineering notes

The physics and the filter are about 800 lines of Rust with no dependencies, compiled to WebAssembly and run in a Web Worker so the page stays responsive. The ray fan is drawn with WebGPU where the browser supports it, and with Canvas 2D where it does not. The page is plain JavaScript. There is no framework, no backend and nothing to install.
